\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{chap:concl}

\section{Summary of Contributions}
This dissertation has presented a three-stage framework for developing wireless foundation models, focusing on self-supervised representation learning, large language model (LLM)-based reasoning, and meta-learning for rapid adaptation. Our work makes the following primary contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DenoMAE: Self-Supervised Multimodal Representation Learning (Chapter 4)}
    We introduced DenoMAE, a novel self-supervised learning framework that learns robust and transferable representations from unlabeled wireless signals. By treating noise as an explicit modality and jointly processing time-domain IQ samples and constellation diagrams, DenoMAE achieves state-of-the-art performance with significantly less labeled data. This work addresses RQ1 and RQ5, demonstrating that multimodal self-supervised learning can produce powerful representations for wireless signals.

    \item \textbf{Plug-and-Play: LLM-Based White-Box Reasoning Classifier (Chapter 5)}
    We proposed a novel framework that leverages the reasoning capabilities of large language models for training-free, open-set classification of wireless signals. This approach, which addresses RQ2, translates statistical signal features into natural language prompts, enabling LLMs to perform classification and provide interpretable decisions.

    \item \textbf{Meta-Task: Rapid Adaptation through Meta-Learning (Chapter 6)}
    To address the challenge of rapid adaptation to new tasks, we introduced the Meta-Task framework, a method-agnostic approach to learnable regularization in few-shot learning. This work, which addresses RQ3, enables models to adapt to new modulation schemes with minimal labeled data, a critical capability for dynamic wireless environments.
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-Domain Validation (Chapter 7 \& 8)}
    Through the analysis of our results, we have shown that the principles of our framework can be extended to cross-domain applications, such as the drone detection paper, which uses vision models for RF signal analysis. This addresses RQ4 and RQ5, demonstrating the potential for cross-modal understanding and knowledge transfer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Key Findings}
Our research has yielded several key findings that advance the state of the art in wireless signal analysis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ1 (Image representations work):} Constellation diagrams are effective visual representations for wireless signals, enabling the use of powerful vision-based models.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ2 (LLMs enable open-set):} Large language models can perform open-set classification of wireless signals, providing a path toward more flexible and intelligent radio systems.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ3 (Meta-learning enables adaptation):} Meta-learning provides an effective mechanism for rapid adaptation to new tasks with minimal labeled data.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ4 (Cross-domain transfer works):} Knowledge can be transferred from the computer vision domain to the wireless domain, as demonstrated by the use of YOLO for RF signal analysis.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ5 (Bidirectional understanding exists):} There is evidence of bidirectional understanding between the signal and image domains, where signal models can understand images and vice versa.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
Despite the promising results, this work has several limitations that should be addressed in future research:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limited to 10-class datasets:} Our experiments were conducted on datasets with a limited number of modulation classes.
    \item \textbf{Computational considerations:} The training of large models and the use of LLMs require significant computational resources.
    \item \textbf{Real-world deployment challenges:} There is a gap between the performance of our models in a controlled lab environment and their potential performance in real-world scenarios with complex channel effects and hardware impairments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Research Directions}
This thesis opens up several exciting avenues for future work, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DenoMAE 2.0 Extensions:} Future work could explore hybrid generative-discriminative extensions of DenoMAE to further improve performance and robustness.
    \item \textbf{DISC-AMC Integration:} The integration of discretized statistics for more efficient LLM reasoning could lead to more parameter-efficient and token-efficient architectures.
    \item \textbf{Combining DenoMAE 2.0 + DISC-AMC:} The synergy between advanced representations from DenoMAE 2.0 and efficient reasoning from DISC-AMC could lead to even more powerful wireless foundation models.
    \item \textbf{Scaling to More Modulation Types:} A natural next step is to scale the pre-training to include a much wider variety of modulation schemes.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Implementation:} Future work should focus on the real-time implementation of these models on resource-constrained hardware.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closing Remarks}
This dissertation has laid the groundwork for a new generation of wireless AI systems based on the principles of foundation models. By integrating self-supervised learning, LLM-based reasoning, and meta-learning, we have demonstrated a path toward building general-purpose, adaptable, and interpretable models for the wireless domain. The vision is to move beyond specialized, black-box classifiers and toward a future of truly cognitive radios that can understand, reason about, and adapt to the complexities of the wireless world.
