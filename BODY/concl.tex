\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{chap:concl}

\section{Summary of Contributions}
This dissertation has presented a three-stage framework for developing wireless foundation models, focusing on self-supervised representation learning, large language model (LLM)-based reasoning, and meta-learning for rapid adaptation. Our work makes the following primary contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DenoMAE: Self-Supervised Multimodal Representation Learning (Chapter 4)}
    We introduced DenoMAE, a novel self-supervised learning framework that learns robust and transferable representations from unlabeled wireless signals. By treating noise as an explicit modality and jointly processing time-domain IQ samples and constellation diagrams, DenoMAE achieves state-of-the-art performance with significantly less labeled data. This work addresses RQ1 and RQ5, demonstrating that multimodal self-supervised learning can produce powerful representations for wireless signals.

    \item \textbf{Plug-and-Play: LLM-Based White-Box Reasoning Classifier (Chapter 5)}
    We proposed a novel framework that leverages the reasoning capabilities of large language models for training-free, open-set classification of wireless signals. This approach, which addresses RQ2, translates statistical signal features into natural language prompts, enabling LLMs to perform classification and provide interpretable decisions.

    \item \textbf{Meta-Task: Rapid Adaptation through Meta-Learning (Chapter 6)}
    To address the challenge of rapid adaptation to new tasks, we introduced the Meta-Task framework, a method-agnostic approach to learnable regularization in few-shot learning. This work, which addresses RQ3, enables models to adapt to new modulation schemes with minimal labeled data, a critical capability for dynamic wireless environments.
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-Domain Validation (Chapter 7 \& 8)}
    Through the analysis of our results, we have shown that the principles of our framework can be extended to cross-domain applications, such as the drone detection paper, which uses vision models for RF signal analysis. This addresses RQ4 and RQ5, demonstrating the potential for cross-modal understanding and knowledge transfer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Key Findings}
Our research has yielded several key findings that advance the state of the art in wireless signal analysis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ1 (Image representations work):} Constellation diagrams are effective visual representations for wireless signals, enabling the use of powerful vision-based models.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ2 (LLMs enable open-set):} Large language models can perform open-set classification of wireless signals, providing a path toward more flexible and intelligent radio systems.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ3 (Meta-learning enables adaptation):} Meta-learning provides an effective mechanism for rapid adaptation to new tasks with minimal labeled data.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ4 (Cross-domain transfer works):} Knowledge can be transferred from the computer vision domain to the wireless domain, as demonstrated by the use of YOLO for RF signal analysis.
    \item \textbf{Answer to RQ5 (Bidirectional understanding exists):} There is evidence of bidirectional understanding between the signal and image domains, where signal models can understand images and vice versa.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
Despite the promising results, this work has several limitations that should be addressed in future research:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limited to 10-class datasets:} Our experiments were conducted on datasets with a limited number of modulation classes.
    \item \textbf{Computational considerations:} The training of large models and the use of LLMs require significant computational resources.
    \item \textbf{Real-world deployment challenges:} There is a gap between the performance of our models in a controlled lab environment and their potential performance in real-world scenarios with complex channel effects and hardware impairments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Research Directions}
The foundation established in this dissertation opens several promising avenues for advancing wireless foundation models. We highlight three particularly impactful directions that build directly on our contributions:

\subsection{DenoMAE 2.0: Hybrid Generative-Discriminative Learning}

DenoMAE 2.0 will address a fundamental limitation of the original framework: the lack of spatial awareness during masked reconstruction. The enhanced architecture will introduce a dual-objective paradigm that will combine traditional reconstruction with position-based patch classification, enabling simultaneous learning of global signal structure and fine-grained local patterns.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{FIGURES/denomae2/flow_con.png}
    \caption{DenoMAE 2.0 architecture overview: The framework will extend masked autoencoding with a classification head that will treat each visible patch as a distinct class based on spatial position. This dual-objective approach will balance reconstruction loss for global feature learning with position-aware classification for capturing local signal patterns, and will yield more robust and transferable representations.}
    \label{fig:denomae2_arch}
\end{figure}

The core innovation will treat each visible (unmasked) patch as a distinct class determined by its spatial position, creating a position-aware learning objective. The combined loss function $\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{rec}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \lambda_{\text{cls}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}$ will balance reconstruction quality with spatial discrimination. This design will encourage the encoder to develop representations that are simultaneously reconstructive (preserving signal structure) and discriminative (capturing position-specific patterns), and will lead to improved generalization across SNR conditions and enhanced transfer learning to out-of-distribution datasets.

Key research directions will include: (1) investigating optimal loss weighting strategies across varying SNR regimes; (2) extending to hierarchical position classification that will capture multi-scale spatial relationships; (3) integrating with Meta-Task's few-shot adaptation mechanisms; and (4) evaluating whether position-aware pretraining will transfer beyond modulation classification to channel estimation, interference detection, and spectrum sensing tasks.

\subsection{DiSC-AMC: Token- and Parameter-Efficient LLM Reasoning}

While Plug-and-Play (Chapter~\ref{chap:plugandplay}) has demonstrated the feasibility of LLM-based wireless classification, practical deployment will face two critical challenges: verbose floating-point feature representations that will consume excessive context windows, and large model requirements that will strain computational budgets. DiSC-AMC~\cite{disc_amc} will address these limitations through a three-stage pipeline designed for resource-constrained deployments.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{FIGURES/disc/method.png}
\caption{Planned DiSC-AMC framework. (1) Signal statistics such as cumulants (c1, c2, c3, ...) will be computed and discretized into bins, represented as different shapes. (2) Exemplars will be pruned using a lightweight top-$k$ shortlisting classifier. (3) The prompt will be structured for efficient, constrained decoding by the LLM.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Stage 1---Statistical Discretization:} Continuous cumulants and spectral features will be mapped into symbolic bins rather than lengthy decimal strings. This discretization strategy will focus LLMs on qualitative statistical patterns rather than noisy numerical precision, aligning wireless signal representation with how foundation models process natural language tokens. The approach will reduce feature verbosity while preserving discriminative information.

\textbf{Stage 2---Dynamic Exemplar Pruning:} A lightweight vision classifier will analyze constellation diagrams to identify the top-$k$ most probable modulation classes, dramatically reducing the context window by including only relevant exemplars. This prefiltering step will be modular and swappable—implementations may use deterministic rules, alternative vision models, or training-free heuristics depending on deployment constraints.

\textbf{Stage 3---Constrained Prompting:} Queries will be reformulated from open-ended classification to a structured multiple-choice format, enforcing label-only predictions and enabling efficient decoding. Combined with selective feature inclusion based on information value, this will yield prompts that are simultaneously compact and interpretable.

The framework will demonstrate that training-free LLM-based classification can operate at practical computational scales with order-of-magnitude reductions in both model size and context length. Future research will include: (1) applying discretization strategies to other statistical wireless features (channel state information, beamforming coefficients); (2) investigating hybrid architectures where learned representations from DenoMAE 2.0 will replace hand-crafted statistical features; (3) extending to multi-task scenarios (joint modulation classification, SNR estimation, and interference detection); and (4) developing adaptive binning strategies that will adjust discretization granularity based on signal conditions.

\subsection{Synergistic Integration: Toward Practical Wireless Foundation Models}

The most promising future direction lies in the principled integration of DenoMAE 2.0 and DiSC-AMC within the dissertation's modular framework. DenoMAE 2.0 provides robust, noise-invariant representations through self-supervised learning; DiSC-AMC enables efficient, interpretable reasoning through token-optimized LLM prompting; and Meta-Task (Chapter~\ref{chap:meta_task}) facilitates rapid adaptation to novel deployment scenarios. Together, these components address complementary aspects of the wireless foundation model challenge:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Representation Learning:} DenoMAE 2.0's dual-objective training produces features that are simultaneously reconstructive (preserving signal structure) and discriminative (capturing class-specific patterns), ideal for both unsupervised pretraining and supervised fine-tuning.
    
    \item \textbf{Efficient Reasoning:} DiSC-AMC's symbolic discretization and dynamic pruning reduce LLM deployment costs by an order of magnitude, making foundation model inference practical for edge devices and spectrum monitoring systems.
    
    \item \textbf{Rapid Adaptation:} Meta-Task's learnable regularization enables few-shot specialization to new modulation schemes or channel conditions with 5--10 labeled examples, avoiding costly retraining.
    
    \item \textbf{Interpretability:} The natural language interface provided by DiSC-AMC complements DenoMAE 2.0's visual reconstructions, offering operators both symbolic explanations and signal-level diagnostics.
\end{itemize}

A concrete integration pathway would: (1) pretrain DenoMAE 2.0 on diverse unlabeled wireless data to learn universal representations; (2) fine-tune the encoder with Meta-Task for few-shot adaptation to deployment-specific classes; (3) extract statistical features from DenoMAE 2.0's reconstructed (denoised) signals; (4) apply DiSC-AMC's discretization and pruning pipeline to these features; (5) use constrained LLM prompting for final classification with interpretable rationales. This end-to-end system would achieve the dissertation's vision: a cost-effective, modular wireless foundation model that approximates world knowledge through strategic component integration rather than massive monolithic training.

\subsection{Additional Promising Directions}

Beyond these primary extensions, several secondary avenues warrant exploration:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scaling to Broader Modulation Taxonomies:} Extending pretraining to 50+ modulation schemes, including emerging waveforms (OTFS, NOMA) and legacy standards (analog FM/AM), to build truly general-purpose encoders.
    
    \item \textbf{Multi-Task Foundation Models:} Training unified architectures that perform modulation classification, SNR estimation, channel equalization, and interference detection simultaneously, leveraging shared representations across tasks.
    
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Edge Deployment:} Optimizing the integrated framework for FPGA and embedded GPU platforms, including quantization-aware training, pruning, and knowledge distillation to meet latency constraints (<10 ms inference) for cognitive radio applications.
    
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Robustness:} Investigating whether DenoMAE 2.0's position-aware learning and DiSC-AMC's symbolic abstractions provide inherent robustness to adversarial perturbations and spoofing attacks in contested electromagnetic environments.
    
    \item \textbf{Cross-Domain Transfer:} Evaluating whether representations learned from communications signals (as in this dissertation) transfer to radar, satellite, or biomedical RF applications, demonstrating true foundation model generality.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closing Remarks}
This dissertation has laid the groundwork for a new generation of wireless AI systems based on the principles of foundation models. By integrating self-supervised learning, LLM-based reasoning, and meta-learning, we have demonstrated a path toward building general-purpose, adaptable, and interpretable models for the wireless domain. The vision is to move beyond specialized, black-box classifiers and toward a future of truly cognitive radios that can understand, reason about, and adapt to the complexities of the wireless world.
