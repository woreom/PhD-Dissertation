\prefacesection{Abstract}

\fancypar{T}{{\sc he}} emergence of foundation models in computer vision and natural language processing has revolutionized artificial intelligence, yet wireless communication systems remain largely dependent on task-specific, narrowly-trained models. This dissertation presents a systematic pathway toward developing wireless physical foundation models—universal representations capable of generalizing across diverse modulation schemes, signal-to-noise conditions, and communication scenarios with minimal task-specific adaptation. We address the fundamental challenge of bridging three traditionally separate research paradigms: unsupervised representation learning, meta-learning, and fine-tuning for downstream tasks.

Our work establishes a three-stage framework for wireless foundation models. First, we develop self-supervised representation learning techniques that extract robust, transferable features from unlabeled wireless signals through multimodal masked autoencoders (DenoMAE and DenoMAE 2.0), treating noise as an informative modality rather than mere corruption. These encoders learn universal wireless signal representations that capture both local patch-level patterns and global signal structure across time-domain and constellation-diagram modalities. Second, we integrate meta-learning strategies (BEpiC, MetaRep, Meta-Task) to enable rapid adaptation to new modulation schemes and channel conditions with minimal labeled examples, effectively learning to learn from wireless data. Third, we pioneer the application of large language model (LLM) integration for open-set automatic modulation classification, demonstrating that DenoMAE-learned encodings serve as semantic features that bridge the wireless signal domain and language model reasoning capabilities.

Our key innovation lies in demonstrating that self-supervised encodings from multimodal autoencoders can function as universal wireless features, enabling zero-shot and few-shot generalization to unseen modulation types through LLM-based reasoning. This represents a paradigm shift from conventional closed-set classification to open-world recognition in wireless systems. We validate our framework across transformer-based architectures (NMformer), meta-learning benchmarks, and semi-supervised learning scenarios (RLPL), consistently demonstrating superior performance in low-data regimes, high-noise conditions, and cross-domain transfer tasks.

This dissertation contributes both theoretical insights and practical methodologies toward realizing wireless foundation models, offering a unified perspective that connects representation learning, meta-learning, and language-guided reasoning for next-generation adaptive communication systems. Our findings suggest that the path to wireless AI requires not merely deeper networks, but fundamentally rethinking how we learn, represent, and reason about wireless signals.

Through a series of interconnected studies, we demonstrate that carefully designed representation learning frameworks can significantly improve performance across diverse domains including wireless communication, image classification, and few-shot learning. Our contributions span three primary research directions: (1) noise-robust representation learning for image classification and wireless communication signals, (2) episodic representation learning for meta-learning, and (3) efficient utilization of unlabeled data through self-supervised, semi-supervised and few-shot learning approaches. The proposed methodologies consistently demonstrate superior performance over existing approaches, particularly in low-data regimes and under challenging noise conditions, offering practical solutions for real-world applications where data acquisition is costly or limited.
