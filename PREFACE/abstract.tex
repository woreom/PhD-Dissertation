\prefacesection{Abstract}
{\noindent\fancypar{T}{{\sc he}} development of foundation models for wireless communications is limited by the cost of training large RF models from scratch, the scarcity of labeled signals, and the fact that models built for one task or dataset rarely carry over to another. This dissertation asks how far wireless foundation-model capabilities can be reached without training a large RF model from scratch, by borrowing world knowledge from vision self-supervision, pretrained language models, and learning-to-learn.\\
It proposes a modular framework organized around three research questions. For representation (RQ1), self-supervised, vision-based encoders (DenoMAE and DenoMAE2.0, co-authored work used here as the representation) give label-efficient features, reaching 82.4\% on matched 10-class data, and the CageDroneRF benchmark shows that vision models work on real over-the-air RF captures (0.986 mAP@0.5 for detection); the representation collapses at extreme noise and transfers weakly to an unseen dataset. For reasoning (RQ2), Plug-and-Play and DiSC-AMC convert signal statistics into compact symbolic prompts with retrieved examples: a fine-tuned 7B model reaches 83.0\% in distribution and 82.5\% out of distribution, and a training-free 7B model reaches 71.9\% at 0.5K tokens, above the proprietary o3-mini baseline at 2.9K tokens. For adaptation (RQ3), Meta-Task's learnable auxiliary task improves few-shot vision benchmarks but gives no net gain on cross-dataset wireless classification.\\
The components compose: DiSC-AMC retrieves over a learned encoder and lifts RadioML accuracy up to eight times over the encoder alone, and the adaptation test runs all three modules together. Across them one finding recurs: the generality of the underlying representation bounds what reasoning and adaptation can add. The domain-specific encoder that wins on matched in-distribution data transfers worse to a new dataset than a generic one, and neither reasoning nor a learned regularizer repairs it. The framework's cost-effectiveness is supported by token- and parameter-efficiency results, not by a complete latency, energy, or monetary comparison, and the results are bounded by single evaluation runs and the absence of published split and run manifests.}

Through a series of interconnected studies, we demonstrate that carefully designed representation learning frameworks can significantly improve performance across diverse domains including wireless communication, image classification, and few-shot learning. Our contributions span three primary research directions: (1) noise-robust representation learning for image classification and wireless communication signals, (2) episodic representation learning for meta-learning, and (3) efficient utilization of unlabeled data through self-supervised, semi-supervised and few-shot learning approaches. The proposed methodologies consistently demonstrate superior performance over existing approaches, particularly in low-data regimes and under challenging noise conditions, offering practical solutions for real-world applications where data acquisition is costly or limited.
